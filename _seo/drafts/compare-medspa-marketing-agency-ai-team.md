Choosing between a medspa marketing agency and an AI marketing team comes down to five questions, not a price: who owns your accounts, who approves what goes live, whether reports end in consults, who checks medical claims, and what happens to the visitor who never calls. An agency is the right call when you need people in the room, such as a photographer, an event, or a weekend crisis. An AI marketing team, managed and monitored by a dedicated human, is the right call for recurring content, follow-up, and reporting, provided that human signs off before anything publishes. The scorecard below grades both.

## Who owns the accounts, the data, and the creative

Whose name is on the Google Business Profile, the ad accounts, the analytics property, and the domain? Good agencies set this up in your name from day one and say so in the contract. Some build on their own accounts and hand over access rather than ownership, which is fine until you leave. Hold an AI marketing team, managed and monitored by a dedicated human, to the same standard: every account in your name, every asset exportable.

## Who approves what goes live, and how fast

Medical aesthetics is regulated, so an owner wants eyes on anything public. At an agency, the loop is usually an email thread, and turnaround depends on the account manager's week. With an AI marketing team managed and monitored by a dedicated human, drafts arrive faster, and that human reviews each one before publication. Either way, insist on a named person signing off, and ask to see the queue where that happens.

## How work is reported

Impressions and reach are easy to report and hard to act on. The numbers an owner can use are consults requested, consults booked, show rate, and the pages they came from. Some agencies already report this way: [Lead to Conversion](https://leadtoconversion.com/medspa/seo/) says its monthly med spa SEO reporting covers rankings, organic traffic, and consultation bookings from organic channels. Ask for a sample report and look for the consult count on page one.

## Compliance review for claims and imagery

Meta's [Health and Wellness advertising standards](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/health-wellness/) cover cosmetic procedures, require adult-only targeting, and bar ads that try to generate negative self-perception. Meta's [Personal Health policy page](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/2489235377779939) adds that ads must not use before and after images to show idealized results. The FTC's [Endorsement Guides FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking) says testimonials must reflect what consumers can generally expect, and that material connections must be disclosed. State medical boards add rules on superlatives, guarantees, and patient photos. This is general information, not legal advice.

Ask each candidate how a before and after image moves from draft to published: who checks the claim, the consent, and the platform policy. A good agency has a checklist and a responsible person. A good AI marketing team, managed and monitored by a dedicated human, has the same, with that human as the gate.

## The medspa marketing agency comparison scorecard

Score each row 0 to 2: 2 if they showed evidence, 1 if they described it, 0 if they could not answer. The higher total tells you which model fits.

| Criterion | Evidence to ask for | Agency, usually | AI team managed and monitored by a dedicated human, usually |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Account ownership | A contract clause naming you as owner | Varies | Varies |
| Approval gate | The sign-off queue | Account manager | The dedicated human |
| Consult reporting | Sample report with consults on page one | Traffic first | By design, so verify it |
| Compliance review | A rejected draft and the checklist behind it | If medspa specialized | The dedicated human |
| Local presence | A photographer, an event, a weekend crisis | Strong | Not offered |
| The visitor who leaves | The treatment page plan for the undecided | Retargeting | Preview tool plus follow-up |

## Questions to ask on the first call

- Which accounts and assets will be in our name, and what do we keep if we leave?
- Who approves content before it publishes, and what is the typical turnaround?
- Show us a sample report. Where are consults requested and booked?
- How do you review before and after images and testimonials against Meta policy, FTC guidance, and our state's rules?
- What do you do for the visitor who reads two treatment pages and never calls?
- Who do we call when something goes wrong on a Saturday?

A local agency knows the referral network and the event calendar. It can staff a launch event, send a photographer, and sit in the room when a provider is nervous about a campaign. ## Signs of a bad fit either way

An agency is a poor fit when the account manager changes every quarter, when reporting stays at impressions after you ask for consults, or when the compliance process is "we have never had a problem." An AI marketing team is supposed to be managed and monitored by a dedicated human, so it is a poor fit when nobody can name that person. The tell is the same: the work is faster than the oversight.

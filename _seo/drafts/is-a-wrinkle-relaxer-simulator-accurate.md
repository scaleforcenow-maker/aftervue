People type the question in a dozen ways. "Is botox simulator accurate" is the most common, with "how will I look after a wrinkle relaxer" and "how would I look with lip filler ai" close behind. The honest answer is that a good preview is accurate about one thing and silent about several others.

This article is general information, not medical advice. Treatment decisions belong with a licensed provider who has examined you.

## What an AI preview actually does

A photoreal preview takes a photo of your own face and edits it. It does not pull up a model or an average face. It softens the lines across your forehead or between your brows, or adds volume to your lips, consistent with the lighting and angle already in the picture. The result looks like a photograph of you because it started as one.

The model is illustrating a direction. It has learned, from many examples, roughly how faces tend to change when a wrinkle relaxer quiets the muscles that fold the skin, or when filler adds volume to a lip. A responsible preview says so on the image: this is an illustration of a possible result, generated by AI. That label is the most accurate sentence in the whole experience.

## What a preview cannot know

Several things that decide your actual result are not in a photograph.

**Dose and placement.** Wrinkle-relaxer results depend on how many units go where. The FDA's information on botulinum toxin products notes that units are specific to each product and cannot be converted between brands. A simulator does not know what product, dose or pattern your provider would choose, so it cannot show the difference between a light touch and a fuller freeze.

**Your anatomy under the skin.** Muscle strength, how your brow sits, skin thickness and how your face moves when you talk all shape the outcome. A still photo captures no movement, and a wrinkle relaxer works on movement.

**Healing and time.** The American Society of Plastic Surgeons describes wrinkle-relaxer results as appearing over a few days, reaching full effect in about a week and typically lasting a few months. Filler can bring swelling that settles over days to weeks. A preview shows one frozen moment with no swelling, bruising or timeline. "What will I look like right after" and "what will I look like in two weeks" are different questions, and the image cannot tell them apart.

**How your skin responds.** Lines etched in over years may soften rather than vanish. A preview that erases them completely shows an optimistic direction, not a likely result for every face.

## Why a preview is not a promise

A preview is a conversation starter. It gives you something concrete to point at and say "more than this" or "less than this", which matters because most people struggle to describe what they want in words.

It is not a forecast. No image model has examined you, and none is in the room when the treatment plan is written. Presenting a preview as a guaranteed outcome is a claim the technology cannot support. The phrasing to look for is "possible", "illustrative" and "likely direction", never a sentence that promises a specific result.

## Consumer apps and practice previews are different things

A generic consumer simulator is usually a filter. It applies the same edit to every face, often leans dramatic because that is what gets shared, and sometimes adds a beauty score. Nobody with clinical training sees the result, and the photo may be stored under the app's terms.

A preview on a practice's own website, or on the iPad in the consult room, differs in two ways that matter. It is tuned to be conservative and labelled as an illustration, because the practice has to stand behind it. And there is a provider nearby, so the image becomes an input to a consult rather than a substitute for one. AmSpa's guidance on good-faith exams describes the first-visit assessment of your history and fitness for treatment that must happen before anything is injected. No preview replaces that exam.

## Questions to bring to your consult

The preview earns its place when it helps you ask better questions. ASPS suggests asking what results are reasonable for you and to see real before and after photos. A few more:

- Which parts of this preview are realistic for my face, and which are not?
- What product and roughly what dose are you planning, and why?
- When should I judge the result, and how long is it likely to last?
- What happens if I want less than the preview shows?
- Who will perform the injection, and what is their training?

## How to read a before/after slider sensibly

Slide it slowly and look at the middle, since the extremes are the least informative frames. Eyes, bone structure and skin texture should be unchanged, and only the treated area should move. A preview that brightens the whole image or smooths skin everywhere is flattering you, not illustrating a treatment. Then ask whether you like the direction, not whether you want exactly that image. The direction is what you take into the room.
